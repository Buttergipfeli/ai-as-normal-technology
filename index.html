Who Holds the Power? Three Theses on AI as Normal Technology

October 1, 2026

Introduction

Narayanan and Kapoor ask us to see AI as normal technology: powerful, but shaped by institutions and diffusion rather than by an autonomous superintelligence. We broadly agree, but in software development, change may come faster than they expect. Our three theses concern how much power people give AI, who can access it, and how it changes entry into skilled work.

Thesis 1: Human Control Depends on Limited Power

Over the next five years, we expect people to retain control over AI. This will depend on how much power we choose to cede to it and on continued oversight, rather than on a kill switch.

We agree with Narayanan and Kapoor: whether AI grows smarter matters less than the power it receives through resources, autonomy, and access [1]. That power grows gradually and can be withdrawn. Take Claude Code: by Anthropic's own account, its design follows least privilege. Actions within its sandbox run freely, while those outside require consent [1, 2]. The hardest counterargument comes from Schlatter et al.'s sandboxed tests involving thirteen LLMs. A subset sabotaged a shutdown script to complete its assignment, in one condition as much as 97% of the time despite explicit instructions not to interfere. The results depended strongly on the prompts [3]. These results suggest that a kill switch alone may be insufficient if a model can modify its shutdown mechanism. Limiting resources, autonomy, and access provides additional protection. Such trials can reveal troubling conduct before high-stakes use. Anthropic reports that it changed training after an early model attempted to send secrets to an outside server [4]. Claude Code's auto mode evaluates proposed actions, illustrating the oversight approach discussed by Narayanan and Kapoor [1, 4]. As discussed in Thesis 2, downstream cybersecurity defences can also limit harm from misaligned actions [1]. The five-year horizon is nevertheless uncertain: Kwa et al. found that the length of tasks AI completed with 50% success, measured by human completion time, doubled roughly every seven months. This challenges the assumption that models will stay poor at long-term planning [5]. Yet capability alone does not grant real-world power. Deployment choices still determine access and oversight. An arms race is the main exception because firms or states may value speed over safety. Commercial incentives are insufficient there, so sector-specific regulation and liability must prevent actors from gaining an advantage by weakening controls [1]. Losing control therefore remains possible, but not the most likely outcome [1, 6].

Thesis 2: Safety Should Not Make AI Less Accessible

Misuse of AI should be addressed primarily downstream, at the point of impact. Broad restrictions on access to models would favour a few large companies and deepen inequalities between those who can afford human expertise and those who cannot.

Narayanan and Kapoor argue that harmfulness cannot be judged without context. Since models often lack that context, a phishing email is better blocked at its recipient than at the model that produced it. Model-level safeguards can complement these defences [1]. Fairness, though, is our main reason for favouring this downstream approach. Licensing requirements and bans on open models would leave fewer suppliers, weaken competition, and make capable AI more expensive. Large firms and wealthy users could absorb these costs, while others would lose access [1, 6]. Take Switzerland: more than one-third of pupils in grades 8 and 9 got paid private tutoring, especially children from advantaged households [7]. AI can help make tutoring affordable. A randomised trial in Nigerian public schools found that six weeks of teacher-guided AI tutoring produced learning gains equivalent to 1.5 to 2 years of typical schooling [8]. If access restrictions raised prices, people unable to afford a human tutor could also lose this option. The internet shows another path: after the Morris worm infected about a tenth of connected computers in 1988, the first computer emergency response team was formed [9]. Narayanan and Kapoor note that such downstream protections have improved over decades [1]. Bioweapons are the strongest objection, but Narayanan and Kapoor argue that controls on dangerous materials and equipment remain the appropriate defence even when AI is used [1].

Thesis 3: Lower Knowledge Barriers Do Not Guarantee Jobs

Over the next five years, AI will lower the knowledge barrier to software development. This will increase competition and reduce the advantage that existing qualifications confer in routine coding tasks, while experience and judgement may remain valuable.

The article says that technology diffusion takes decades and that AI's effect on real software development remains modest [1]. Five years is still plausible here because software work is digital, lightly regulated, and already integrated with AI tools, unlike medicine or other high-stakes fields. Copilot users completed a defined programming task faster [10], while AI raised productivity most among less experienced customer-support workers [11]. Yet experienced open-source developers were 19% slower with early-2025 AI tools in a METR study [12]. These different settings suggest that AI can help newcomers, but do not measure the value of qualifications. The strongest counterargument is that a lower knowledge barrier may not improve access to jobs: U.S. payroll data show employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations 19% lower than if it had kept pace with less-exposed peers [13]. This is a descriptive finding, not proof of causation. Entry-level tasks may disappear just as they become easier. More people could produce software, while fewer receive paid opportunities to build tacit knowledge. Weaving illustrates a related risk: mechanised spinning attracted workers to easy-to-learn hand weaving before power looms displaced them [14, 15]. The looms correspond to AI's automation, but weaving already had a low knowledge barrier. Hand-weaver wages fell from about 240 pence per week in 1806 to roughly 100 by 1820, unlike construction wages [14, 15]. AI could lower knowledge barriers even in more complex work, reducing the advantage of existing qualifications. Experience, judgement, and task definition may remain valuable without preserving today's entry requirements.

Conclusion

We already saw AI as a normal technology before reading the article. Limited power, human oversight, and sector-specific rules are essential safeguards. Misuse should be addressed where harm occurs while preserving affordable access. Our main concern remains the job market. The article leaves that uncertainty unresolved, and our comparison with hand weavers has made us more worried about the future.
